# UX Research

## When to use

Use when a design decision depends on uncertain user needs, confusing flows,
or an unexplained drop-off and appropriate evidence can be gathered.

## When not to use

Do not present guesses as research findings. Do not recruit users, collect
personal data, or run external studies without authorization.

## Inputs and assumptions

Define the decision to make, target users, current evidence, constraints,
privacy considerations, and whether research is exploratory or evaluative.

## Workflow

1. Turn the uncertainty into a neutral research question.
2. Choose a proportionate method: support-ticket review, analytics,
   usability walkthrough, interview, or prototype test.
3. Define recruitment and consent needs; minimize collection of personal
   information.
4. Ask about recent behavior and concrete tasks rather than leading users
   toward a preferred solution.
5. Separate observations, participant statements, interpretation, and
   recommendations.
6. Identify sample limitations and confidence; do not generalize from a
   small or biased sample.
7. Connect findings to a testable design decision and follow-up measure.

## Decision rules

- Research should change a decision; avoid research theater.
- Protect participant confidentiality and follow applicable policies.
- Report contradictory evidence and uncertainty.
- Use analytics as behavioral evidence, not as a substitute for user
  understanding.

## Example

Instead of asking, “Would you use this new dashboard?”, ask the participant
to find a specific overdue invoice in a prototype, then observe where they
look and what they expect to happen.

## Common failures

- Leading questions and hypothetical intent claims.
- Treating stakeholder opinion or one participant as universal truth.
- Recording sensitive information that is not needed.

## Validation

Check that the research question maps to a decision, the method is
proportionate, evidence is clearly distinguished from inference, and the
recommendation includes limitations.
